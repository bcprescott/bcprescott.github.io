# This Isn't an Anti-AI Post (And It Won't Be)
 
The goal of this post is _not_ to convince you to stop using AI tools. I use AI daily, and you probably are too. If you're _not_ using it in some capacity, you're likely making things way harder on yourself than they need to be.
 
The point of this post is about _how_ we use these tools, not _whether_ we should. There's a meaningful difference between those two questions, and I think the conversation around AI adoption tends to collapse them into one. You're either "pro-AI" or you're a luddite. That's a bit of a false choice, and it's a bit lazy (ha).
 
So, I thought it would be fun to put together something practical here... a set of principles, habits, or ways of thinking that might help you (or your team) use AI tools intentionally, without giving up the skills and critical thinking that make you amazing at what you do in the first place.


![intelligence](/public/images/losingyourself/ai-spectrum.svg)
 
As you read through this article, keep a few things in mind:
 
- This post is intended to spark thought, not just provide opinions
- I'll be writing follow-up posts that go deeper on a few of these topics
- This is a conversation I'm genuinely interested in, so I'd love to hear where you agree, disagree, or have your own experiences to share

## Intentional (and Healthy) AI Tool Usage
 
There's a phrase that keeps popping up in discussions about AI, and it goes something like: "AI is just a tool, it's all about how you use it". While that's _technically_ true, I think it oversimplifies the situation a bit. I mean, a hammer is also "just a tool," but you don't use a hammer for 8+ hours a day in a way that fundamentally changes how you think about and approach problems.
 
AI tools, especially the exciting generative ones like Large Language Models (LLMs - yes, there are others), are a different kind of tool. They don't just _do_ a task for you, they can do the _thinking_ part of the task too, and that matters more than _we_ think.
 
So what does intentional usage actually look like? Here are a few approaches I've been trying to follow myself:
 
**Know what you're offloading.** Before you jump into an AI tool, take a second to identify what part of the work you're handing off. Is this some basic formatting task, or is some complex reasoning, analysis, or creative problem-solving? There's nothing wrong with offloading the boring stuff, that's kind of the whole point. But if you find yourself consistently offloading the _hard_ stuff, you might want to take a breath and determine if you're using it to create actual value, or if you're contributing to the AI slop machine.
 
**Use AI to accelerate, not to replace your process.** I like to think of AI as a really fast first draft generator or a brainstorming partner. It can get you from zero to "something on the page" incredibly quickly. This even applies to some of the hard stuff! But if you're accepting outputs without any old fashioned critical thinking, you're just a middleman between the AI and whoever receives the final product. You've turned yourself into a human AI gateway. One of the rules highlighted by creative thinkers working through this same challenge is to always apply your own judgment and editing to AI-generated output, rather than treating it as a finished product [1].
 
**Be honest with yourself about _why_ you're using it.** Are you using AI because it genuinely makes this task better or faster? Or are you using it because you don't feel like doing the work? Both are valid in different contexts, but only one of them is a pattern you should be concerned about if it becomes the default. 
 
**Verify, always.** AI tools are confident. They are _always_ confident. That confidence has zero correlation with accuracy or 'correctness' in the context of your task. Are the outputs something you'd be willing to stake your reputation on? How have others approached the same task? Do you have a baseline to compare against? Using AI may have just made you look worse if you aren't reviewing or modifying the outputs.

## Exercising the Skills AI Could Atrophy
 
This is the section I've been wanting to write for a while, because I think it captures the main purpose of this whole post.
 
Here's a simple analogy I keep coming back to. Think about what happened when GPS proliferated everywhere. Before GPS, if you drove somewhere a few times, you _learned_ the route, sometimes using a physical map (wtf is that, right?). You then built a mental map. You understood the relationships between places and landmarks. Now? I've driven to places dozens of times and still couldn't tell you the route without pulling up Google Maps. That capability didn't disappear overnight, it atrophied because I stopped exercising it.

This example might be a bit extreme, as many of you are probably asking "are you thinking we need to learn map reading in case GPS goes down forever?", in which case I'd agree with the sentiment and say we'd probably have bigger issues. However, the intent behind the example stands. 
 
![gps](/public/images/losingyourself/brain-gps-comparison.svg)
 
AI tools pose a similar risk, but for _cognitive_ skills rather than navigational ones. Research from Polytechnique Insights highlights that from a neurological standpoint, widespread use of generative AI carries a real risk of overall cognitive atrophy and a loss of brain plasticity [6]. Put simply, the "use it or lose it" principle applies to your brain just as much as it does to your muscles.
 
The sad part is this isn't purely theoretical. Developers on forums like Reddit (an obvious scientific journal) are already reporting noticeable declines in their ability to write code from scratch after relying too heavily on AI coding assistants for extended periods [4]. The skills you don't practice are the skills you lose. That's not an opinion, that's just how it works.
 
So, what do we do about it? I've started trying to think about this in the context of **AI fitness** - deliberate practicing of skills that AI could otherwise handle for you. A few general thoughts:
 
- **Write/type things from scratch periodically.** Whether it's code, documentation, emails, or analysis, force yourself to do the full process without AI assistance on a regular basis. It doesn't have to be every time. But it should be often enough that you're maintaining the muscle. Don't forget how to write good, k?
 
- **Do the math (or the logic, or the research).** If you're using AI to summarize articles, try _actually_ reading a few of them yourself each week. If you're using AI to debug code, try working through the logic manually sometimes.
 
- **Practice forming an opinion _before_ asking AI.** This is subtle, but I think it's important. If you're writing something or solving a problem, try to form your own perspective before consulting an AI tool. Once you've seen the AI's answer, it's really hard to think independently about the same question. There's a subconcious anchoring effect that happens (bias, anyone?).
 
- **Set "no-AI" blocks in your schedule.** This might apply to the more extreme users out there, but having even a few hours a week of working without AI tools can help you stay sharp. Think of it like going for a run. You're not doing it because you LOVE running (at least I'm not), you're doing it because the exercise itself has value.
 
The point isn't to be anti-tool. The point is to make sure that _you_ remain the capable one in the partnership, not just the glorified button pusher.

## Avoiding Collective Overreliance
 
Everything we've talked about so far has been at the individual level, but I think the organizational dynamics are actually more interesting (and more concerning).
 
When one person becomes overly reliant on AI, they lose some sharpness. When an entire _team_ does, you start losing institutional knowledge, mentorship quality, and the ability to critically evaluate AI-generated work, because nobody on the team is practicing those skills anymore.
 
![dev](/public/images/losingyourself/juniordevflow.png)
 
Research on AI adoption in organizations suggests that thoughtful implementation is critical to maintaining trust and ensuring these tools actually enhance rather than diminish team capabilities [7]. Most organizations are implementing AI tools with the singular goal of "move faster, be more efficient!" and not giving much thought as to what gets lost along the way.
 
Here are a few things I think teams and leaders should be considering:
 
**Don't let AI become a crutch for junior team members.** This is probably the one that concerns me the most. Junior developers/analysts/writers _need_ to struggle through problems to build their skills. If the default is "just ask ChatGPT," they may produce acceptable output in the short term, but they're not developing the deep understanding that comes from having to struggle through a hard problem. The short-term productivity gain could come at the cost of long-term team readiness. Those that know me know I love saying "embrace the suck", and I feel it applies here.
 
**Create space for AI-free collaboration.** Some of the best problem-solving happens in whiteboard sessions, pair programming, or just talking through a problem with a colleague. If every question gets routed to an AI tool first, you lose the peer-to-peer knowledge exchange that builds strong teams. Research on AI in nonprofit organizations has highlighted that over-reliance on AI can weaken workplace engagement and reduce this kind of peer-to-peer exchange [9].
 
**Be transparent about where AI was used.** This doesn't need to be a formal audit trail, though in some industries it probably should be (healthcare comes to mind [3]). But within a team, it's helpful to know whether a deliverable was primarily human-generated or AI-assisted. Not as a judgment, just as context for how much additional review it might need. We're all in the same boat and we'll get to our destination faster if we're honest with each other.
 
**Resist the pressure to optimize _everything_.** Not every task needs to be unbelievably efficient. Some of the "slower" ways of doing things, like code reviews, manual analysis, writing things out longhand, have value beyond their direct output. They build understanding, catch edge cases, and develop skills. Efficiency isn't _always_ the most important metric, even if it feels like it should be.

## AI Gets Better, Balance Gets Harder
 
If I'm being honest, the trajectory of the industry makes me a little uncomfortable.
 
As AI tools become more capable (and they _will_ continue to become more capable), the temptation to offload more and more cognitive work is only going to increase. When AI can write code that's better than what most developers can produce from scratch, what's the argument for doing it yourself? When it can generate analysis that would take you hours in a matter of seconds, why bother with the manual approach?
 
I'll bring us back to my somewhat lame GPS analogy for a second. You don't practice navigating without GPS because you think GPS is going to disappear. You practice because there's value in _you_ (as a contributing member of society) understanding how to get from point A to point B, because it gives you a mental model of the world that makes you better at everything else, including knowing when the GPS is sending you down a weird route.
 
The same logic applies here. You don't practice writing code from scratch because you think AI coding assistants are going away. You practice because the deep understanding you build by doing so makes you better at _using_ the AI tools, better at evaluating their output, and better at solving the novel problems that the AI hasn't seen before.

![capability](/public/images/losingyourself/capability.png)

As these tools improve, the value of the remaining people who can think independently, evaluate critically, and bring genuine expertise to the table is only going to go _up_. The people who will thrive aren't the ones who can prompt the best, but are the ones who understand a domain deeply enough to know when AI is wrong, when the approach is flawed, or when the problem needs to be reframed entirely. 
 
The more powerful these tools get, the more it matters that you actually know what you're doing with them. That's true of chainsaws, it's true of airplanes, and it's definitely going to be especially true of AI.
 
---
 
## Final Thoughts
 
My entire post really boils down to one thing: **use AI tools enthusiastically, but don't offload your ability to think critically.**
 
Be excited about what these tools can do. Use them to move faster, explore ideas, automate the tedious stuff, and take on something you might not have tackled otherwise.
 
But also be intentional. Keep your skills sharp. Think before you jump immediately into using an AI tool. **Trust but verify *FIRST*.** And every once in a while, do the thing the hard way, and not because you have to, but because the act of doing it is what keeps you capable of doing it (or even helps keep your mental sanity). 
 
The goal isn't to resist AI. It's to make sure you're growing alongside the tools, not being replaced by your own workflow. The people who get this balance right are going to have a massive advantage once the next generation of AI capabilities comes and everyone else is trying to figure out how to evaluate outputs they no longer understand.

**TL;DR** - In a world dominated by AI, don't forget the value in being human.
